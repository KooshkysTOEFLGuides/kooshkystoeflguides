Meanings and usage

The core definition and examples are always included. Additional notes appear only when they are accurate and useful.

Meaning 1

Officially introduce and enforce

transitive verb /ɪmˈpoʊz/ neutral to formal; very common in academic, legal, political, economic, and policy contexts
🔊 Hear transitive verb pronunciation

to officially introduce a rule, tax, punishment, restriction, or other requirement and make people obey or accept it

In simpler words: officially make a rule or requirement apply

Typical pattern: impose + tax, fine, ban, restriction, limit, penalty, sentence, or requirement

Natural examples

  1. The government imposed stricter limits on industrial pollution.
  2. The court imposed a substantial fine on the company.
  3. Several countries imposed new taxes on imported goods.
  4. The university imposed a temporary ban on parking near the construction site.
  5. Authorities imposed restrictions on water use during the drought.
  6. The treaty imposed strict limits on the country's military activities.
  7. The judge imposed a sentence of three years in prison.
  8. New regulations impose additional safety requirements on manufacturers.

Nuance and implication

Central nuance: Impose emphasizes authority and lack of free choice: a government, court, institution, or other authority makes something apply to people.

Usual implication: The people affected may not agree with or welcome the rule, tax, restriction, or punishment.

Compared with “recommend”: To recommend something is to advise people to do it. To impose it is to make it compulsory or officially apply it.

Grammar notes

How to use it
Put the rule, tax, punishment, or restriction directly after impose.
What can follow it
Common objects include tax, fine, penalty, ban, restriction, limit, requirement, condition, sanction, and sentence.
Common preposition
When naming the people or organization affected, use on or upon: impose a tax on consumers, impose restrictions on factories.
Position in a sentence
The passive form is also common: A fine was imposed on the company.

Useful patterns

impose + tax or fee + on + person or group

Use this when an authority officially requires people to pay money.

Example: The city imposed a new fee on property developers.

impose + restriction or limit + on + activity

Use this when an authority officially limits what people can do.

Example: The government imposed restrictions on commercial fishing.

impose + fine or penalty + on + person or organization

Use this when an authority officially punishes someone.

Example: Regulators imposed a heavy fine on the company.

impose + ban

Use this when an authority officially prohibits something.

Example: The city imposed a temporary ban on outdoor fires.

be imposed on + person, group, or activity

Use the passive form when the rule or punishment is the main focus.

Example: Strict limits were imposed on groundwater use.

Common wrong usage

Wrong: The government imposed to companies stricter regulations.

Correct: The government imposed stricter regulations on companies.

Put the thing being imposed directly after impose, and use on before the people or organizations affected.

Wrong: The court imposed the company a fine.

Correct: The court imposed a fine on the company.

The normal pattern is impose something on someone.

Wrong: The government imposed people to follow the new rule.

Correct: The government required people to follow the new rule.

Do not use impose + person + to + verb. Use impose a rule on someone, or use require someone to do something.

Meaning 2

Force something unwanted on someone

transitive verb /ɪmˈpoʊz/ neutral to formal; common in academic discussions of policy, economics, society, education, and ethics
🔊 Hear transitive verb pronunciation

to force an unwanted burden, idea, condition, or responsibility on someone

In simpler words: force someone to accept or deal with something

Typical pattern: impose + burden, cost, pressure, condition, belief, responsibility, or demand + on + someone

Natural examples

  1. Parents should be careful not to impose their own career ambitions on their children.
  2. The new reporting requirements impose a significant burden on small businesses.
  3. Critics argued that the policy imposed unnecessary costs on local communities.
  4. A teacher should not impose personal political beliefs on students.
  5. The project imposed additional responsibilities on employees who were already overworked.
  6. Rapid population growth can impose severe pressure on public services.
  7. The agreement imposed several difficult conditions on the smaller company.
  8. The disaster imposed enormous financial costs on the region.

Nuance and implication

Central nuance: This sense focuses on making someone carry, accept, or deal with something they did not freely choose.

Usual implication: The imposed thing is often difficult, unwanted, restrictive, expensive, or unfair.

Compared with “offer”: Something offered can be accepted or refused. Something imposed is forced on the person or group.

Grammar notes

How to use it
Put the burden, idea, condition, or responsibility directly after impose.
What can follow it
Common objects include burden, cost, pressure, condition, responsibility, demand, belief, value, and expectation.
Common preposition
Use on or upon before the person or group receiving the unwanted thing: impose a burden on taxpayers.
Position in a sentence
The passive is common when focusing on the affected person or group: Extra responsibilities were imposed on staff.

Useful patterns

impose a burden on + person or group

Use this when something creates extra difficulty, work, or expense.

Example: The paperwork imposes a heavy burden on small businesses.

impose costs on + person or group

Use this when a decision causes people to pay or lose money.

Example: Environmental damage can impose substantial costs on future generations.

impose pressure on + person, system, or resource

Use this when something creates strain or difficulty.

Example: Rapid urban growth imposes pressure on transportation systems.

impose beliefs or values on + someone

Use this when someone tries to force another person to accept particular ideas.

Example: Schools should educate students without imposing a particular political ideology on them.

impose conditions on + someone

Use this when someone must accept certain requirements.

Example: The lender imposed strict conditions on the borrower.

Common wrong usage

Wrong: Parents should not impose their children their beliefs.

Correct: Parents should not impose their beliefs on their children.

Use impose something on someone.

Wrong: The policy imposed many pressures to small businesses.

Correct: The policy imposed considerable pressure on small businesses.

Use on, not to. Pressure is also commonly uncountable in this meaning.

Wrong: She imposed me to accept her opinion.

Correct: She tried to impose her opinion on me.

Do not use impose someone to do something. Use impose something on someone.

Meaning 3

Burden someone with your presence or demands

transitive verb /ɪmˈpoʊz/ neutral; often used politely when discussing favors, hospitality, personal space, or unwanted attention
🔊 Hear transitive verb pronunciation

to force your presence, attention, or demands on someone when they may not want them

In simpler words: cause someone inconvenience by asking too much or staying when not wanted

Typical pattern: impose on + someone, or impose yourself on + someone

Natural examples

  1. I did not want to impose on my neighbors by asking them to drive me to the airport.
  2. She felt that staying for another week would impose on her hosts.
  3. He was careful not to impose himself on colleagues who were already extremely busy.
  4. I hope I am not imposing, but could I use your phone for a moment?
  5. They offered us dinner, but we did not want to impose on their hospitality.
  6. She disliked imposing on friends for help unless it was absolutely necessary.
  7. The visitor imposed himself on the family and stayed far longer than expected.

Nuance and implication

Central nuance: This sense often expresses concern that your request, presence, or behavior may inconvenience another person.

Usual implication: It is frequently used in polite language when someone wants to avoid taking advantage of another person's kindness.

Compared with “respect someone's space”: Respecting someone's space means avoiding unwanted demands or intrusion. Imposing on someone means creating an unwanted burden through your presence or requests.

Grammar notes

How to use it
Use impose on someone when your request or presence may burden them. Use impose yourself on someone when you force your company or attention on them.
What can follow it
After impose on, name the person or group affected. You can also say impose on someone's hospitality or generosity.
Common preposition
On is essential in this pattern: impose on someone.
Position in a sentence
This meaning often appears in negative or polite expressions such as I don't want to impose or I hope I'm not imposing.

Useful patterns

impose on + someone

Use this when asking a favor or staying with someone might inconvenience them.

Example: I do not want to impose on you, so I can find a hotel instead.

impose on someone's hospitality

Use this when you worry that you are accepting too much kindness from a host.

Example: We decided to leave early rather than impose on their hospitality.

impose yourself on + someone

Use this when a person forces their presence or attention on someone else.

Example: He had a habit of imposing himself on people who wanted to be left alone.

I hope I'm not imposing

Use this polite expression before or after making a request that may inconvenience someone.

Example: I hope I'm not imposing, but could I stay here tonight?

Common wrong usage

Wrong: I don't want to impose you.

Correct: I don't want to impose on you.

When impose means inconvenience someone through a request or your presence, use impose on someone.

Wrong: He imposed himself to his neighbors.

Correct: He imposed himself on his neighbors.

The correct pattern is impose yourself on someone.

Wrong: I hope I don't impose to ask for another favor.

Correct: I hope I'm not imposing by asking for another favor.

A natural pattern is be imposing by + -ing form.

Morphology

Word family

Only common and educationally useful family members are included.

countable noun

imposition

/ˌɪmpəˈzɪʃən/

an unfair or inconvenient demand or burden placed on someone

Relationship: This noun can refer to a burden or demand that has been imposed on someone.

Pattern: an imposition on + person or group

Usage note: It is especially common when talking about inconvenience or an unreasonable demand.

  1. Requiring employees to work every weekend would be a considerable imposition.
  2. She apologized for the imposition and thanked her neighbors for helping.
  3. The additional paperwork was seen as an unnecessary imposition.

adjective

imposing

/ɪmˈpoʊzɪŋ/

large, impressive, and likely to attract attention because of appearance or size

Relationship: This adjective is historically related to impose but has developed the separate common meaning of impressive in size or appearance.

Pattern: imposing + building, structure, figure, entrance, or appearance

Usage note: Do not assume that imposing means forcing something in this adjective use.

  1. The museum occupies an imposing stone building in the center of the city.
  2. The mountain presents an imposing sight from the valley below.

Meaning relations

Synonyms and antonyms

Only clear and useful relationships are included.

Close synonyms

enforce

transitive verb · Officially introduce and enforce

Shared meaning: Both involve authority and compulsory rules.

Difference: Enforce means make sure an existing law or rule is obeyed. Impose means introduce or place the rule, restriction, or punishment on people in the first place.

Example: Police are responsible for enforcing traffic laws.

levy

transitive verb · Officially introduce and enforce

Shared meaning: Both can mean officially require a tax or financial charge.

Difference: Levy is a more specialized formal verb used mainly for officially imposing taxes, fees, or other financial charges. Impose can be used much more broadly.

Example: The city levied a tax on vacant properties.

force

transitive verb · Force something unwanted on someone

Shared meaning: Both involve lack of free choice.

Difference: Force is broader and often focuses on making a person perform an action. Impose usually focuses on placing a rule, burden, belief, condition, or cost on someone.

Example: The circumstances forced the company to reduce production.

burden

transitive verb · Force something unwanted on someone

Shared meaning: Both can describe causing someone to carry an unwanted difficulty.

Difference: Burden emphasizes giving someone difficult responsibilities, costs, or problems. Impose is broader and can also refer to rules, beliefs, restrictions, or conditions.

Example: The complicated process burdens small businesses with unnecessary paperwork.

intrude

intransitive verb · Burden someone with your presence or demands

Shared meaning: Both can describe unwanted interference with another person.

Difference: Intrude emphasizes entering someone's space, privacy, or activity without permission. Impose on emphasizes causing inconvenience by asking too much or taking advantage of someone's hospitality.

Example: I did not want to intrude on their private conversation.

Direct or useful antonyms

lift

transitive verb · Officially introduce and enforce

Opposition: To impose a restriction or ban is to introduce it; to lift it is to officially remove it.

Usage note: This is a strong opposite specifically for restrictions, bans, sanctions, and similar measures.

Example: Authorities lifted the restrictions after conditions improved.

remove

transitive verb · Officially introduce and enforce

Opposition: Impose can mean place a requirement or restriction on someone, while remove means take that requirement or restriction away.

Example: The government removed several unnecessary restrictions.

Precision

Words learners may confuse

enforce

transitive verb

Why it is confusing: Both commonly appear in discussions of laws, rules, and government authority.

Key difference: Impose means introduce or place a rule, tax, punishment, or restriction on someone. Enforce means make sure an existing rule or law is obeyed.

impose: The city imposed a new parking restriction.

enforce: Police began enforcing the new parking restriction on Monday.

propose

transitive verb

Why it is confusing: The words look somewhat similar but represent very different stages of decision-making.

Key difference: Impose means make something apply without giving the affected people a choice. Propose means suggest something for consideration.

impose: The government imposed a new tax.

propose: The government proposed a new tax, but parliament had not yet approved it.

imposing

verb versus adjective

Why it is confusing: Imposing looks like the -ing form of impose but also has an established adjective meaning that learners may not expect.

Key difference: Impose is a verb meaning force or officially place something on someone. Imposing is commonly an adjective meaning large, impressive, and visually powerful.

impose: The court imposed a heavy fine on the company.

imposing: The courthouse is an imposing stone building.

Word history

Etymology

Only confidently documented details are shown.

Origin

Impose comes through French from Latin elements meaning to put or place upon.

Development

The idea of placing something upon another thing developed into the modern meanings of placing a rule, burden, duty, tax, or demand on someone.

Memory link

Think of impose as putting something on someone: a tax, rule, burden, belief, or demand.
