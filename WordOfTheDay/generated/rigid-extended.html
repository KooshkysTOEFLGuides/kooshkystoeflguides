Meanings and usage

The core definition and examples are always included. Additional notes appear only when they are accurate and useful.

Meaning 1

Stiff and unable to bend

adjective /ˈrɪdʒɪd/ neutral to formal; common in science, engineering, biology, medicine, and descriptions of materials
🔊 Hear adjective pronunciation

stiff and not able to bend, change shape, or move easily

In simpler words: hard to bend

Typical pattern: rigid + structure, frame, material, surface, or support

Natural examples

  1. The building is supported by a rigid steel frame.
  2. Unlike flexible cartilage, bone is relatively rigid.
  3. The container must be rigid enough to protect the equipment during transportation.
  4. The insect's rigid outer covering helps protect its internal organs.
  5. Engineers needed a lightweight but rigid material for the structure.
  6. The cold temperature made the plastic more rigid and easier to break.
  7. A rigid surface does not absorb impact as easily as a flexible one.
  8. The telescope requires a rigid support to prevent small movements from affecting the measurements.

Nuance and implication

Central nuance: Rigid emphasizes resistance to bending or changing shape.

Usual implication: Rigidity can provide strength and stability, but it can also make an object more likely to crack or break under certain forces.

Compared with “flexible”: A flexible object can bend or change shape relatively easily. A rigid object strongly resists bending or deformation.

Grammar notes

How to use it
Use it before a noun, as in a rigid frame, or after a linking verb, as in The frame is rigid.
What can follow it
It commonly describes materials, structures, surfaces, containers, supports, and parts of the body.
Position in a sentence
Words such as highly, relatively, completely, and sufficiently can appear before rigid.

Useful patterns

a rigid + material or structure

Use this for something that strongly resists bending or changing shape.

Example: The device is enclosed in a rigid plastic shell.

remain rigid

Use this when something continues to keep its shape despite pressure or movement.

Example: The frame must remain rigid under heavy loads.

rigid enough to + verb

Use this when a certain level of stiffness is necessary for a purpose.

Example: The panel is rigid enough to support the weight.

become more or less rigid

Use this when the stiffness of a material changes.

Example: Some plastics become more rigid at low temperatures.

Common wrong usage

Wrong: The metal is very rigidity.

Correct: The metal is very rigid.

Use the adjective rigid after is. Rigidity is a noun.

Wrong: The frame is too rigid to bend easily, which means it is very flexible.

Correct: The frame is too rigid to bend easily, which means it is not very flexible.

Rigid and flexible have opposite meanings in physical descriptions.

Wrong: The material has a rigid.

Correct: The material is rigid.

Rigid is an adjective, not a countable noun.

Meaning 2

Strict or difficult to change

adjective /ˈrɪdʒɪd/ neutral to formal; common in academic, organizational, social, political, and educational contexts
🔊 Hear adjective pronunciation

very strict, fixed, or unwilling to change, even when different circumstances might require flexibility

In simpler words: too fixed or strict

Typical pattern: rigid + rule, system, schedule, structure, approach, hierarchy, or attitude

Natural examples

  1. A rigid schedule can make it difficult for employees to respond to unexpected problems.
  2. The school abandoned its rigid rules and gave teachers greater freedom in the classroom.
  3. Critics argued that the government's rigid approach prevented it from adapting to changing economic conditions.
  4. The organization once had a rigid hierarchy in which junior employees had little influence.
  5. A rigid interpretation of the law may produce unfair results in unusual cases.
  6. The researchers warned against using a rigid classification system for such a complex phenomenon.
  7. His rigid views made productive discussion difficult.
  8. Some companies are replacing rigid working hours with more flexible arrangements.

Nuance and implication

Central nuance: This sense transfers the physical idea of something that cannot bend to rules, systems, plans, or attitudes that cannot easily adapt.

Usual implication: The word is often negative because too little flexibility can make a system or person unable to respond well to new circumstances.

Compared with “flexible”: A flexible rule, system, or approach can be adjusted when circumstances change. A rigid one remains fixed and allows little adjustment.

Grammar notes

How to use it
Use it before nouns such as rule, schedule, system, approach, hierarchy, structure, or attitude, or after a linking verb.
What can follow it
It frequently describes policies, institutions, ways of thinking, organizational structures, and procedures.
Position in a sentence
Too rigid and overly rigid are common when criticizing a lack of flexibility.

Useful patterns

a rigid system or structure

Use this for an organization or arrangement that allows little change.

Example: The rigid administrative system made reform extremely slow.

a rigid schedule

Use this for a timetable that allows little or no adjustment.

Example: Parents with young children may struggle with a rigid work schedule.

a rigid approach to + noun or -ing form

Use this for a method that does not adapt easily to different situations.

Example: A rigid approach to teaching may not meet the needs of every student.

too or overly rigid

Use this when strictness or lack of adaptability creates problems.

Example: The regulations were criticized for being overly rigid.

rigid views or beliefs

Use this for opinions that a person is unwilling to reconsider.

Example: Rigid beliefs can make compromise difficult.

Common wrong usage

Wrong: The company follows rigidly rules.

Correct: The company follows rigid rules.

Use the adjective rigid before a noun such as rules.

Wrong: The schedule is rigid to changes.

Correct: The schedule is rigid and difficult to change.

Rigid is not normally followed by to changes in this meaning.

Wrong: The manager was rigidly about working hours.

Correct: The manager was rigid about working hours.

Use the adjective rigid after was. Rigidly is an adverb.

Wrong: A rigid policy is always better because everyone follows the same rule.

Correct: A rigid policy applies fixed rules but may be unsuitable when circumstances differ.

Rigid does not mean fair or effective; it emphasizes lack of flexibility.

uncountable noun

rigidity

/rɪˈdʒɪdəti/

the quality of being physically stiff or of being too fixed and unwilling or unable to change

Relationship: This noun names the physical stiffness or lack of flexibility described by rigid.

Pattern: the rigidity of + object or system

Usage note: It is normally uncountable.

  1. The rigidity of the structure helps it resist strong winds.
  2. Critics blamed the system's rigidity for its inability to respond quickly to the crisis.
  3. Excessive organizational rigidity can discourage innovation.

adverb

rigidly

/ˈrɪdʒɪdli/

in a strict, fixed, or inflexible way

Relationship: This adverb describes an action performed in a rigid or inflexible way.

Pattern: rigidly + enforce, follow, apply, control, or define

  1. The organization rigidly enforced its traditional procedures.
  2. The categories should not be applied too rigidly because some cases overlap.

Meaning relations

Synonyms and antonyms

Only clear and useful relationships are included.

Close synonyms

stiff

adjective · Stiff and unable to bend

Shared meaning: Both can describe something that does not bend easily.

Difference: Stiff is the more common everyday word for something difficult to bend or move. Rigid is more formal and often suggests stronger resistance to changing shape.

Example: The cardboard becomes stiff when several layers are glued together.

inflexible

adjective · all senses

Shared meaning: Both can describe something that does not adapt or bend easily.

Difference: Inflexible is especially common for rules, plans, attitudes, and people that cannot or will not change. Rigid can describe both this figurative lack of flexibility and literal physical stiffness.

Example: The company's inflexible policies made it difficult for employees with family responsibilities.

strict

adjective · Strict or difficult to change

Shared meaning: Both can describe rules or systems that place strong limits on behavior.

Difference: Strict means demanding that rules be followed carefully. Rigid goes further by suggesting that the rules or approach allow too little adjustment when circumstances change.

Example: The laboratory follows strict safety procedures.

fixed

adjective · Strict or difficult to change

Shared meaning: Both can describe something that does not change easily.

Difference: Fixed means established and not changing. Rigid often adds a negative idea that the lack of change creates difficulties or prevents adaptation.

Example: The course follows a fixed timetable throughout the semester.

Direct or useful antonyms

flexible

adjective · all senses

Opposition: Flexible describes something that can bend physically or adapt to changing circumstances, while rigid describes something that strongly resists bending or change.

Example: The flexible material can bend without breaking.

adaptable

adjective · Strict or difficult to change

Opposition: An adaptable person, system, or approach can change to suit new circumstances, while a rigid one changes with difficulty.

Example: Successful organizations need adaptable systems that can respond to unexpected conditions.

Precision

Words learners may confuse

rigorous

adjective

Why it is confusing: Both are formal adjectives beginning with rig- and can describe demanding systems or procedures, but their meanings are different.

Key difference: Rigid means physically stiff or unwilling or unable to change. Rigorous means extremely careful, thorough, or demanding.

rigid: The rigid schedule allowed almost no changes.

rigorous: The researchers used a rigorous method to test the hypothesis.

strict

adjective

Why it is confusing: Both can describe rules and systems that strongly limit behavior.

Key difference: Strict emphasizes strong rules and careful enforcement. Rigid emphasizes lack of flexibility and often suggests that adjustment is difficult or impossible.

rigid: The rigid policy could not be adapted to unusual cases.

strict: The laboratory has strict rules about handling dangerous chemicals.

Word history

Etymology

Origin

Rigid comes from Latin rigidus, meaning stiff or hard.

Memory link

The original idea of physical stiffness helps explain the figurative meaning: a rigid rule or attitude cannot easily bend or adapt.
