Meanings and usage

The core definition and examples are always included. Additional notes appear only when they are accurate and useful.

Meaning 1

Overused and no longer original

adjective /traɪt/ neutral to formal; usually disapproving and common in discussions of writing, speech, art, advertising, and ideas
🔊 Hear adjective pronunciation

expressed or used so often that it no longer seems interesting, original, or effective

In simpler words: used so much that it feels boring or unoriginal

Typical pattern: trite + phrase, expression, saying, remark, observation, idea, or argument

Natural examples

  1. The essay relies on trite phrases instead of developing its ideas clearly.
  2. His advice to simply follow your dreams sounded trite to the students.
  3. The advertisement was criticized for using trite images of success and happiness.
  4. The speaker avoided trite statements and supported her argument with specific evidence.
  5. Although the message was sincere, its wording seemed trite and predictable.
  6. The novel contains several trite observations about love and relationships.
  7. Teachers encouraged students to replace trite expressions with more precise language.
  8. The conclusion that technology has both advantages and disadvantages can sound trite unless it is developed with specific examples.

Nuance and implication

Central nuance: Trite does not simply mean common or simple. It means something has been repeated so often that it has lost much of its originality, freshness, or emotional effect.

Usual implication: The speaker or writer is criticizing the expression or idea as predictable and insufficiently thoughtful.

Compared with “original”: An original expression or idea feels fresh or distinctive. A trite one feels familiar because it has been used too many times.

Grammar notes

How to use it
Use it before a noun, as in a trite expression, or after a linking verb, as in The conclusion sounds trite.
What can follow it
It commonly describes phrases, expressions, sayings, remarks, observations, ideas, arguments, themes, and advice.
Position in a sentence
It often appears after sound, seem, become, or feel when judging language or an idea.

Useful patterns

a trite phrase or expression

Use this for wording that has been repeated so often that it no longer sounds fresh.

Example: The introduction begins with a trite phrase about the importance of education.

a trite remark or observation

Use this for a comment that is familiar and lacks originality.

Example: The article ends with the trite observation that change is inevitable.

sound or seem trite

Use this when an idea may be sincere but its wording feels overused.

Example: It may sound trite, but careful preparation really does improve performance.

avoid trite + noun

Use this when recommending more original or precise language.

Example: Academic writers should avoid trite expressions when a more precise statement is possible.

Common wrong usage

Wrong: The phrase is trite because it is easy to understand.

Correct: The phrase is trite because it has been used so often that it no longer sounds original.

Trite does not mean simple or easy to understand. It means overused and lacking freshness.

Wrong: The author made a tritely argument.

Correct: The author made a trite argument.

Use the adjective trite before a noun such as argument.

Wrong: This expression is very trite because nobody has used it before.

Correct: This expression is very original because nobody has used it before.

A trite expression is familiar because it has been used many times.

Wrong: The speaker trited several common ideas.

Correct: The speaker repeated several trite ideas.

Trite is an adjective, not a verb.

Morphology

Word family

Only common and educationally useful family members are included.

uncountable noun

triteness

/ˈtraɪtnəs/

the quality of being overused and no longer original or interesting

Relationship: This noun names the quality of being trite.

Pattern: the triteness of + phrase, idea, remark, or theme

Usage note: It is much less common than trite.

  1. The triteness of the slogan made it easy to ignore.
  2. Critics complained about the triteness of the film's message.

adverb

tritely

/ˈtraɪtli/

in an overused, predictable, or unoriginal way

Relationship: This adverb describes something said or expressed in a trite way.

Pattern: tritely + say, conclude, observe, or describe

Usage note: It is uncommon; trite is much more useful for learners.

  1. The article tritely concludes that there are two sides to every issue.
  2. The character tritely describes love as the answer to every problem.

Meaning relations

Synonyms and antonyms

Only clear and useful relationships are included.

Close synonyms

clichéd

adjective · Overused and no longer original

Shared meaning: Both describe something that has lost originality because it has been used too often.

Difference: Clichéd describes something that uses familiar clichés or predictable conventions. Trite is very close in meaning but can apply more broadly to remarks, ideas, observations, and advice that have become dull through repetition.

Example: The film was criticized for its clichéd characters and predictable ending.

hackneyed

adjective · Overused and no longer original

Shared meaning: Both mean overused and no longer fresh or original.

Difference: Hackneyed is a stronger and somewhat more literary word for language or ideas that have become dull through excessive use. Trite is more common and straightforward.

Example: The writer relied on hackneyed images of heroic leaders and helpless victims.

banal

adjective · Overused and no longer original

Shared meaning: Both can criticize ideas or remarks for lacking originality.

Difference: Banal emphasizes that an idea or remark is boring because it is ordinary and lacks originality. Trite more specifically emphasizes loss of freshness through repeated use.

Example: The interview produced several banal comments about the importance of teamwork.

stale

adjective · Overused and no longer original

Shared meaning: Both can describe language or ideas that have lost their freshness.

Difference: Stale can figuratively describe ideas, jokes, or language that no longer feel fresh. Trite specifically suggests that repeated use has made them unoriginal and ineffective.

Example: The campaign relied on stale slogans that no longer attracted voters.

Direct or useful antonyms

original

adjective · Overused and no longer original

Opposition: Original describes an idea or expression that is fresh or distinctive, while trite describes one that has lost its originality through repeated use.

Example: The student offered an original explanation that none of the other essays had considered.

fresh

adjective · Overused and no longer original

Opposition: A fresh idea or expression feels new and interesting, while a trite one feels familiar and overused.

Usage note: This opposite applies to the figurative meaning of fresh.

Example: The author brings a fresh perspective to a familiar topic.

Precision

Words learners may confuse

cliché

adjective versus countable noun

Why it is confusing: The two words express almost the same basic idea but have different grammatical roles.

Key difference: Trite is an adjective describing something as overused and unoriginal. A cliché is a countable noun referring to an overused phrase, idea, or image itself.

trite: The conclusion sounds trite.

cliché: The phrase time heals all wounds is often treated as a cliché.

trivial

adjective

Why it is confusing: Both are negative adjectives beginning with tri-, but they criticize different qualities.

Key difference: Trite means overused and unoriginal. Trivial means unimportant or too minor to deserve much attention.

trite: The speech contained several trite remarks about success.

trivial: The disagreement concerned a trivial detail and was resolved quickly.

Word history

Etymology

Only confidently documented details are shown.

Origin

Trite ultimately comes from Latin tritus, meaning rubbed or worn.

Development

The physical idea of something being worn down through repeated use developed into the modern figurative meaning of an expression or idea being worn out through repetition.

Memory link

A trite phrase is like a path that has been walked on so many times that it feels worn out.
