# STEP 2 — REUSABLE IDEA BANK

## Prompt

You are an expert writer of reusable idea banks for the TOEFL Speaking Interview task.

Create a comprehensive idea bank for:

**MAIN TOPIC:** Neighborhood, City Life, and Community

My original seed subtopics were:

- neighborhood description
- city life
- countryside life
- urban vs rural living
- local facilities
- public parks
- green spaces
- public services
- safety
- noise
- crowds
- traffic
- neighbors
- sense of community
- community events
- community centers
- local problems
- neighborhood improvement
- city planning
- quality of life
- access to services

Here is the approved expanded coverage map and vocabulary plan:

Use the completed `words.json` output supplied with this prompt as the approved Step 1 material.

The approved structure should guide your organization, but do not assume it covers every possible idea. My original subtopics and the approved sections are **minimum coverage requirements, not strict boundaries**.

Add another idea category when you notice a genuinely useful TOEFL interview angle that is still missing. Do not add categories merely to make the bank look larger.

## Purpose of the idea bank

The ideas should help B1–B2 students answer many different questions without memorizing one complete answer for each question.

Each idea must be:

* reusable across several possible questions;
* specific enough to produce meaningful development;
* simple enough to explain naturally in spoken English;
* suitable for a response of approximately 30–45 seconds;
* distinct from the surrounding ideas;
* realistic for students and ordinary adults;
* adaptable to personal, social, educational, professional, or public contexts when relevant.

The idea bank should not merely repeat facts about the topic. It should give students useful **answer angles**.

## Coverage

Include a broad and balanced mixture of ideas involving, when relevant:

* personal preferences;
* habits and routines;
* memorable experiences;
* convenience and practicality;
* enjoyment and emotional effects;
* physical or mental benefits;
* skill development;
* identity and self-expression;
* confidence and independence;
* social connection;
* family relationships;
* children and education;
* costs and accessibility;
* obstacles and disadvantages;
* risks and unintended consequences;
* fairness and social inequality;
* environmental or ethical concerns;
* traditional versus modern approaches;
* individual responsibility;
* institutional or government responsibility;
* short-term versus long-term effects;
* present conditions and future developments.

Do not force irrelevant political, environmental, technological, or child-related ideas into the topic. Use them only when they create natural speaking content.

Include positive, negative, and balanced ideas. Do not make the entire bank promotional or one-sided.

## Idea quality

Avoid generic claims such as:

* “It is good for people.”
* “It has many advantages.”
* “It makes life better.”
* “People can learn many things.”

Replace them with precise, reusable claims, such as:

* the activity creates structure in a person’s week;
* the habit reduces decision fatigue;
* the option is accessible to people with limited time;
* the experience gives people a shared topic of conversation;
* the policy may help one group while creating costs for another.

Avoid repeating the same underlying idea with slightly different wording.

For example, “reduces stress,” “helps people relax,” and “makes people feel calmer” should not become three separate ideas unless each one presents a genuinely different mechanism or context.

### Individually developed contexts

Write every `context` specifically for its own `idea`. Never create one category-level explanation and copy it into several idea objects. Never use a generic context template with only a noun, activity, person, place, or weather condition substituted.

Each context must add information that belongs to that heading alone. It should normally supply a particular mechanism, realistic example, consequence, limitation, contrast, or condition. A reader who hides the heading should still be able to infer which idea the context develops.

Before returning the JSON:

1. Compare every context string across the complete bank. No two may be identical.
2. Compare the underlying sentence frames, not just exact wording. If several contexts differ only through substituted details, rewrite them from scratch.
3. Check that the context does not merely restate its heading or attach the same broad paragraph to every idea in a category.
4. Reject generic filler that could support unrelated ideas, such as “This affects choices at home, school, or work” or “The result depends on local conditions and available resources.”
5. Make examples concrete without inventing statistics, oddly specific facts, or implausible experiences.

Treat duplicated or templated contexts as a failed output even when the idea headings themselves are distinct.

## Relationship to the vocabulary plan

Use the approved vocabulary and collocations naturally where they fit, but do not turn the idea bank into a vocabulary demonstration.

Do not force advanced vocabulary into every item.

The ideas should remain understandable to B1–B2 students.

## Output format

Return one valid UTF-8 JSON object only, with no Markdown fence or commentary. Save it as `ideas.json` in the topic directory. It must use this exact versioned shape:

```json
{
  "schema_version": 1,
  "topic": "Neighborhood, City Life, and Community",
  "sections": [
    {
      "title": "Thematic category",
      "ideas": [
        {
          "idea": "A regular activity can create structure in a person’s week.",
          "context": "If someone attends a class every Saturday morning, the activity gives the week a clear rhythm. It can also motivate the person to organize other responsibilities around it."
        }
      ]
    }
  ],
  "coverage_audit": [
    {"section": "Thematic category", "coverage": ["Seed or added subtopic"]}
  ],
  "added_categories": ["Any category added beyond Step 1"]
}
```

Every object must contain exactly the displayed fields. Use an empty `added_categories` array when none were added. Arrays must contain values of the displayed kind—never Markdown embedded inside strings.

The heading should express the reusable idea clearly. The “Use in context” section should explain, illustrate, qualify, or apply the idea rather than simply repeat it.

## Quantity

Aim for **at least 10 genuinely distinct thematic categories**. Broad topics will often justify **12–18 categories**. Do not collapse substantial seed or Step 1 subtopics merely to keep the bank compact.

Aim for **100 or more strong ideas**. Broad topics will often justify **120–180 or more** when genuinely distinct answer angles remain.

For each major category, normally provide approximately 8–14 strong ideas, but vary the number naturally. Do not mechanically give every category exactly 10 ideas or use symmetrical section sizes merely to reach the total.

These are strong depth expectations, not quotas or stopping points. Continue beyond them when useful content remains, but do not pad the bank with weak, generic, or repetitive ideas merely to increase the count.

You may stop below a preferred target when reaching it would require restating the same mechanism, adding generic claims, forcing irrelevant social angles, or dividing one natural category artificially. A finished bank must still contain at least **8 real categories and 90 strong ideas**. Treat those lower numbers as anti-thinness floors, not satisfactory targets.

Before returning the JSON, count the categories and ideas silently. If an anti-thinness floor is not met, identify under-covered seed or added subtopics and develop more distinct ideas. If a preferred target is not met, first search carefully for genuinely distinct answer angles, but stop rather than pad when none remains.

Then perform a **mechanical-pattern audit**. List the number of ideas in every category internally. If one identical count occurs in 60% or more of categories, the bank is mechanically sized: reconsider and regenerate the affected categories according to how many genuinely distinct answer mechanisms each subject merits.

Do not evade this audit by randomly deleting strong ideas, splitting one idea into superficial variants, adding generic claims, or creating artificial categories. A rich category may naturally contain many more ideas than a narrow one. Quantity must vary as a consequence of content judgment, not cosmetic count manipulation. Do not return the JSON until this audit passes.

## Additional requirements

* Make the ideas varied in grammatical form.
* Use concrete examples from ordinary life.
* Include some nuanced ideas with limitations or conditions.
* Include useful contrasts.
* Include both individual and broader social angles when appropriate.
* Do not write complete 45-second sample answers.
* Do not generate interview questions.
* Do not reproduce the vocabulary lists.
* Do not produce HTML.
* Do not include instructions addressed to students.
* Do not ask me follow-up questions.

Record the coverage audit and any new categories only in the corresponding JSON fields. Escape quotation marks and line breaks according to JSON syntax. Do not leave placeholders in the result.
